# Cross-resource reproducibility and incremental validity of colorectal cancer dependency maps

**Running title:** Validity of colorectal dependency maps

**Author:** Byungwoong Yoo

## Abstract

Cross-resource agreement in cancer dependency maps need not imply added information beyond existing models. We evaluated reproducibility, historical clinical precedence and pharmacologic association using independent three-dimensional (3D) resources and a lineage-matched two-dimensional (2D) comparator. Aggregate profiles from 18 Broad colorectal 3D screens, 67 Broad colorectal 2D screens and 81 Sanger colorectal models without annotated Broad overlap were compared across 15,008 genes. The strongest dependency decile contained 1,501 genes per 3D resource, with 763 shared genes (odds ratio [OR], 17.89); 645/763 (84.53%) were also highly ranked in 2D. Sanger concordance was 0.330 with Broad 3D and 0.353 with Broad 2D (Spearman difference, −0.0232; conditional gene-bootstrap 95% interval, −0.0330 to −0.0131). Among 994 selected strong-2D genes, all-three-profile membership was associated with historical Phase III-or-higher precedence after adjustment for 2D dependency strength and essentiality (OR, 4.22; 95% confidence interval, 1.19–14.97; nominal P=0.0259), but not Phase II-or-higher precedence (OR, 1.35; 0.59–3.11; P=0.482). The separate 3D-specific class showed no significant enrichment. Across 26 mapped inhibitory drugs, Broad 3D dependency correlated with independent metastatic-colorectal organoid drug sensitivity (ρ=0.420; P=0.0326), but its correlation advantage over 2D was uncertain (difference, 0.125; drug-bootstrap 95% interval, −0.086 to 0.362). These retrospective, aggregate comparisons identify reproducible dependencies and a selective historical-precedence association without establishing a generic 3D-specific advantage. Resource agreement, incremental information, clinical development history and pharmacologic association should be interpreted separately.

**Keywords:** colorectal cancer; CRISPR dependency; organoids; cross-resource reproducibility; historical clinical precedence; drug sensitivity; incremental validity

## Introduction

Functional genetic screens provide a route from cancer genomes to experimentally measured vulnerabilities. Their translational use depends not only on whether a perturbation changes cellular fitness, but also on whether that effect is reproducible, informative beyond existing models and relevant to a therapeutic intervention. Target-prioritization studies have therefore combined CRISPR dependencies with molecular context, tractability and clinical information rather than treating fitness alone as a sufficient criterion [1,2]. A dependency map can support one of these objectives without satisfying all of them.

Independent screening initiatives offer an opportunity to distinguish robust biological signal from resource-specific variation. Comparisons and integration of the Broad and Sanger conventional cell-line datasets established substantial cross-study agreement while also addressing differences in screening and processing [3,4]. These studies make reproducibility an essential starting point, but they do not make every agreement statistic a measure of incremental model value. Strongly deleterious perturbations may recur across resources because they capture shared fitness requirements, including requirements already represented in a comparator dataset.

The expansion of functional genomics into three-dimensional (3D) systems creates a timely setting in which to examine this distinction. Han and colleagues identified growth-context-dependent vulnerabilities using cancer spheroids and comparisons with two-dimensional (2D) and in-vivo systems [5]. The 2026 Broad NextGen and Sanger tumour-organoid resources broaden the range of patient-relevant cancer models available for large-scale dependency analysis [6,7]. The Broad study emphasizes complementary subtype coverage, preserved transcriptional programs and contributions of both medium and growth format; the Sanger study provides an extensive organoid dependency resource. These are not claims that every aggregate statistic must favour 3D systems. An external comparison should therefore assess the information provided by the resources without recasting their contributions as an assertion of universal superiority.

Translation introduces a further distinction. Historical advancement of drugs directed at a gene reflects biological knowledge, chemical tractability, development investment and clinical decision-making. It is not a prospective test of a newly generated dependency map. Pharmacologic responses are closer to intervention outcomes but differ from genetic loss of function because exposure, target engagement, polypharmacology and cellular context intervene. Dependency-informed therapeutic modelling already integrates genetic and drug-response information [8]. An independent metastatic-colorectal patient-derived organoid (PDO) resource offers a separate pharmacology dataset [9], but a cohort-level association with drug sensitivity remains different from patient-specific treatment prediction.

Here, we ask what replication across the two new 3D resources validates beyond a lineage-matched 2D dependency profile. We analyse colorectal cancer (CRC) within a fixed, published comparison-gene universe and separate cross-resource agreement, information not already captured by strong 2D dependencies, historical clinical precedence and external pharmacologic association. We retain both the positive and non-supportive results, including a transparently corrected clinical model. The aim is not to rank culture formats globally, introduce a new predictor or nominate individual therapeutic targets, but to define the scope of conclusions supported by this cross-resource evidence.

## Results

### Independent 3D resources share strongly ranked CRC dependencies

The final analysis included 18 Broad CRC 3D screens, 67 Broad CRC adherent 2D screens and 81 Sanger CRC models after exclusion of models annotated as overlapping Broad. Broad counts refer to screens and Sanger counts to models; neither denotes independently sampled patients. Restriction to the Broad Figure 5 comparison universe and complete values in the three aggregate profiles yielded 15,008 shared genes (Figure 1). The analysis compared source-processed profiles rather than jointly reprocessing raw CRISPR counts.

At the locked strongest-decile threshold, each 3D resource contributed 1,501 genes, of which 763 were shared. The Fisher overlap odds ratio was 17.8883 (Figure 2). Thus, strong dependency ranks were reproducible across the two resources despite differences in model collections and screening pipelines. Previously computed descriptive sensitivities also showed overlap at the strongest 5% and 20%: 421 of 751 genes per list (OR, 53.8408) and 1,363 of 3,002 (OR, 5.2601), respectively. These thresholds are sensitivity descriptions, not separate confirmatory hypotheses or alternative opportunities to select a preferred clinical result.

### Most replicated 3D dependencies were also highly ranked in 2D

The 763-gene 3D intersection decomposed into 645 genes also within the Broad 2D strongest decile and 118 outside that decile. Consequently, 84.53% of the intersection was shared by all three aggregate profiles. The locked code labels these groups UNIVERSAL_2D_BOTH3D and THREE_D_SPECIFIC_CONSENSUS, respectively. A separate TWO_D_ONLY group contained 349 genes within the Broad 2D strongest decile but outside both 3D strongest deciles (Figure 2).

These labels are operational rank-based classifications. “Universal” means shared by the three aggregate profiles, not essential in every model or cancer. Similarly, the 118-gene “3D-specific” class is defined by threshold membership; it is not a set of statistically demonstrated culture-format interactions. Genes supported by only one 3D resource and other membership patterns were retained in the shared universe but were not part of the three-class clinical contrasts. The large all-three-profile component shows why a high 3D–3D overlap alone cannot establish how much information was absent from the 2D profile.

### Sanger concordance did not preferentially favour the Broad 3D profile

Across the 15,008-gene filtered universe, the Sanger CRC 3D profile had Spearman correlations of 0.3299897370 with Broad CRC 3D and 0.3531986912 with Broad CRC 2D. Their directly evaluated difference, 3D minus 2D, was −0.0232089542, with a conditional gene-bootstrap 95% interval of −0.0330344 to −0.0131187 (Figure 3). Pearson correlations were 0.5109250 and 0.5181936, respectively. The earlier, unfiltered 16,318-gene comparison had the same ordering of Spearman correlations (0.4762673 and 0.4944996), but is not a replacement primary analysis.

In the observed CRC cohorts, the aggregate Sanger 3D dependency profile was therefore not more concordant with Broad 3D than with the lineage-matched Broad 2D profile. The difference was small in absolute terms. Its bootstrap interval characterizes resampling of genes conditional on these aggregate profiles; it does not incorporate recruitment of new model cohorts or identify a causal effect of culture format. The comparison was lineage-matched, not a paired experiment in identical models.

### Historical clinical precedence differed by class and endpoint

Clinical annotations were drawn from Open Targets release 26.06 [10], restricted to the CRC disease root and descendants. The locked comparison between the all-three-profile class and TWO_D_ONLY comprised 994 genes: 645 and 349, respectively. Historical Phase II-or-higher precedence was present in 36/645 versus 9/349 genes (5.58% versus 2.58%; unadjusted OR, 2.2332; two-sided Fisher P=0.03661). Phase III-or-higher precedence was present in 27/645 versus 3/349 (4.19% versus 0.86%; OR, 5.0388; P=0.002901).

After the endpoint-orientation correction described in Methods, the same logistic model adjusted for continuous Broad CRC 2D dependency and the Open Targets-derived essentiality indicator. The adjusted all-three-profile association was not significant for Phase II-or-higher precedence (OR, 1.3487761406; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.5853139188–3.1080707618; P=0.4823844923). For Phase III-or-higher precedence, the association was positive and nominally significant (OR, 4.2187090214; 95% CI, 1.1886118578–14.9733537404; P=0.0259240472; Figure 4). These models contained 45 and 30 events, respectively. The original approval-coded endpoint had only three events and severe separation; its adjusted estimate was not interpretable.

The positive Phase III-or-higher result concerns replication among selected genes already strongly ranked in 2D, not enrichment of the exclusive 3D class. In the separate comparison of THREE_D_SPECIFIC_CONSENSUS against TWO_D_ONLY, Phase II-or-higher events were 4/118 versus 9/349 (OR, 1.3255; P=0.7461), and Phase III-or-higher events were 0/118 versus 3/349 (P=0.5751). Neither comparison supported enrichment. This absence of significant evidence, especially with few events, is not evidence of equivalence or absence of biological relevance.

The corrected findings do not support a narrative in which adjustment eliminated every clinical association. They instead identify an endpoint-specific historical-precedence association for the all-three-profile group, with substantial uncertainty, alongside a non-significant Phase II-or-higher association and sparse exclusive-3D comparisons. No causal or prospective clinical interpretation follows from these results.

### Independent PDO pharmacology provided exploratory association but uncertain added value

The recovered external Data S5 workbook contained 211 PDO rows and 62 columns [9]. The locked column-processing rule retained 51 analyzable single-agent-labelled DSS series after its specified regimen exclusions. Of these labels, 41 mapped unambiguously to Open Targets drug molecules, 35 had an eligible inhibitory-mechanism annotation, and 26 drugs retained at least one target within the filtered dependency universe. The 211 rows are organoid records, not 211 independent patients. Analyses summarized DSS across available PDO rows for each drug and did not perform patient-clustered inference.

For the 26-drug analysis using the strongest mapped target dependency, correlations with median PDO DSS were 0.4200791 for Broad CRC 3D (P=0.03263), 0.2947730 for Broad CRC 2D (P=0.14378) and 0.3176291 for Sanger CRC 3D (P=0.11383). The paired difference between the two Broad correlations was +0.1253061, with a drug-bootstrap 95% interval of −0.08612 to +0.36223 (Figure 5). Although every leave-one-drug-out point estimate for this difference was positive (+0.05854 to +0.16221), the bootstrap interval did not establish an advantage over 2D.

Results depended on target summarization. Taking the median dependency across each drug’s retained targets gave correlations of 0.2428916, 0.1959577 and −0.0236423 for Broad 3D, Broad 2D and Sanger 3D, respectively (P=0.23185, 0.33736 and 0.90873). The Broad correlation difference was +0.0469339 (95% interval, −0.17130 to +0.26653). Among the 21 drugs with at most three retained targets, median-target correlations were 0.4954385, 0.3174717 and 0.1851375, respectively; the Broad difference was +0.1779667 (95% interval, −0.04968 to +0.44444). The corresponding strongest-target sensitivity also had a difference interval spanning zero (Supplementary Table S6).

The drug-level analysis was preferred over preliminary target-level summaries because it avoided assigning the same multi-target drug response to several ostensibly independent target observations. Earlier seven-gene single-target and 72-gene inclusive analyses are retained as exploratory results in the supplement, not additional confirmations. Overall, Broad CRC 3D dependency showed an exploratory association with independent PDO drug sensitivity, but a statistically robust incremental advantage over lineage-matched 2D was not established. The Sanger correlations did not provide a comparably consistent replication across the two target summaries.

## Discussion

This cross-resource comparison separates a reproducible dependency signal from the different claims that might be built on it. The two 3D CRC resources agreed strongly in their most highly ranked dependencies. Most of that intersection was also highly ranked in the 2D comparator. Aggregate Sanger concordance did not preferentially favour Broad 3D. Nevertheless, replication in both 3D resources was associated with historical Phase III-or-higher precedence within the selected strong-2D comparison set after the locked adjustment. External PDO pharmacology supplied a further, exploratory association for Broad 3D without establishing a statistically robust advantage over 2D. These are compatible findings, not a single verdict for or against 3D models.

Cross-study agreement has long been an important component of dependency-map evaluation [3,4]. The present results extend that question to two large organoid-era resources while retaining a lineage-matched conventional-model reference. The 645-gene all-three-profile overlap explains why replication can be compelling without being format-specific. Conversely, the 118-gene exclusive-3D class shows that overlap with 2D is not complete. Neither observation establishes whether an individual gene is an actionable selective vulnerability. A cohort mean suppresses information about rare molecular subtypes and heterogeneous responses, whereas target-prioritization frameworks explicitly incorporate such context [1,2]. Our rank-based classifications answer a narrower, aggregate question.

The concordance result should not be interpreted as contradicting the biological contributions of the source studies. Work in spheroids has identified context-dependent phenotypes [5], and the two 2026 resources provide additional models and biological contexts [6,7]. A resource can expand subtype coverage or reveal a dependency absent from older collections even when its average gene ranking is not closer to another resource’s average than a 2D reference is. Global rank concordance is therefore not a complete criterion for choosing models for a particular biological question. In particular, the present analysis did not reproduce the source studies’ matched culture-medium or growth-format experiments.

The corrected clinical result adds information that a uniformly negative interpretation would miss. Within a selected subset of strong 2D dependencies, genes also strongly ranked in both 3D resources were more likely to have Phase III-or-higher historical precedence. This association persisted in the specified model, but its CI was wide and the endpoint was secondary and retrospective. The corresponding Phase II-or-higher association was not significant. Different endpoints summarize different histories of development, and neither a nominally significant later-stage association nor a non-significant earlier-stage association justifies a broad claim about clinical success. In addition, the adjustment was limited to two covariates; it did not remove development investment, target tractability, publication intensity or all biological confounding.

The clinical comparison also has an important selection boundary. The adjusted model contrasted genes strong in all three profiles with genes strong only in Broad 2D. It excluded strong-2D genes supported by exactly one 3D resource. Its coefficient is consequently a conditional association between these selected classes, not the average benefit of adding any 3D evidence across all genes. The exclusive-3D class did not show significant clinical-precedence enrichment, but there were too few later-stage events to treat the comparison as an equivalence test. “Not enriched in this analysis” should not become “not therapeutically relevant.” Historical clinical evidence can also favour long-studied targets whose development preceded the datasets under evaluation.

The pharmacology layer addresses a different endpoint in an independent data source. Dependency-informed drug-response modelling has biological and methodological precedent [8]; this analysis instead compares the aggregate information supplied by three fixed resource profiles. Genetic disruption and pharmacologic modulation are not interchangeable interventions. The strongest-target summary assumes that a drug’s most dependent annotated target can be informative, but the maximizing target may differ between resource profiles. The median-target summary makes a different assumption and can dilute a dominant target effect. Their differing results, particularly for Sanger, indicate that the inferred association is sensitive to how annotations are summarized. The analysis did not establish exposure-specific target engagement, resolve all off-target activity or test patient-level predictions.

Several limitations constrain inference. First, the Broad collections were imbalanced at 18 3D and 67 2D screens. Matching lineage did not match patient, genotype, subtype, prior treatment or growth rate. The source collections also differ in medium, library and processing. Thus, culture format is not isolated as the cause of the observed differences. Excluding the Sanger models annotated as overlapping Broad reduced known resource overlap but is not a genomic audit of every possible shared donor or model derivative.

Second, the correlations operate across genes in cohort-mean profiles. Gene-bootstrap intervals condition on these profiles, do not quantify model-sampling uncertainty and may underrepresent dependence between genes participating in shared complexes or pathways. Rank-defined classes can also be sensitive near their boundaries. The published comparison-gene filter was held fixed rather than optimized, but it does not make our all-three-profile class synonymous with common essentiality. The existing q5 and q20 calculations provide descriptive context only; they are not independent replications of the clinical hypothesis.

Third, the original approval endpoint was too sparse for stable inference. The retained historical-stage dictionary included withdrawal among the highest coded stages and used maximum recorded stage. Accordingly, these annotations describe the specified development-history proxy, not current FDA or EMA approval, trial efficacy or continued availability. The secondary clinical P values were nominal, not multiplicity-adjusted evidence of a prospectively specified treatment-prediction hypothesis. The endpoint-orientation correction was a necessary implementation repair, not a new endpoint or a justification for selecting the positive Phase III-or-higher result.

Fourth, drug-level aggregation avoids one form of target-level pseudo-replication but does not make every observation fully independent. Drugs may share targets and mechanisms, and multiple PDO rows may derive from the same patient. The locked analysis used medians across available rows, not patient-weighted estimates or patient-clustered resampling. It also averaged same-labelled library columns rather than restricting to the strict 24-drug harmonized panel. Different drugs can therefore be summarized over different PDO sets and concentration ranges. These issues limit both the precision and generalizability of the pharmacology comparison.

The practical implication is to keep the validation question aligned with its comparator and statistical unit. Agreement between resources supports reproducibility of a specified representation. Evidence not present in a 2D rank profile is a separate property. Historical clinical precedence and independent pharmacologic association supply additional, non-equivalent information. Here, the coexistence of strong overlap, a selective historical-precedence association and uncertain pharmacologic increment argues for evaluating these dimensions separately. It neither establishes generic 3D superiority nor implies that patient-relevant 3D systems lack value.

## Methods

### Study design and analysis state

This was a retrospective secondary analysis of public, source-processed CRISPR dependency data, target-development annotations and an independent PDO drug-sensitivity dataset. CRC was selected for the lineage-matched comparison. The initial source exploration, definitive filtered comparison, pharmacology summaries and endpoint correction preceded this manuscript. The q10 analysis was locked for the correction and first draft, not prospectively registered before all data inspection. No additional thresholds, disease scopes, outcomes, regression specifications or favourable subsets were introduced during manuscript preparation. Clinical-stage outcomes are secondary retrospective endpoints; drug-response analyses are exploratory.

The evidence sequence is preserved in the target-stability repository. The definitive filtered workflow was executed as run 35407055037; target-level PDO results as run 35408854800; the preferred drug-level results as run 35409997973; and the explicit numeric-endpoint correction as run 35414237376. The correction is pinned to commit 0a61f7940a65f323a1f48b7e7ba581931f41c1c9. The older handoff remains an audit record and is superseded by the corrected q10 clinical results where their interpretations differ.

### CRC model selection and aggregation

Broad screen metadata and processed gene effects were accessed through the DepMap portal’s Breadbox interface for the NextGen resource [6]. CRC was identified from the reported Oncotree lineage labels. The 3D cohort required IsNextGen to be true and ScreenType to equal 3DO; the 2D comparator required IsAdherent2D to be true. Only screens represented in the dependency matrix were included. Source gene-effect values were averaged across the selected screens within each cohort, yielding 18 3D and 67 2D screens. No donor-level pairing between these cohorts was imposed.

Sanger processed fitness values and availability annotations came from supplementary_table_6_revision.csv and the data_availability sheet of supplementary_table_2_revision.xlsx supplied with the organoid study [7]. Models required CRISPR availability and a colorectal primary-tumour label. Those annotated as overlapping Broad were excluded using overlap_Broad_DepMap. Within each retained sample_ID–gene combination, LFC values across available library rows were averaged before averaging across models. This yielded 81 retained Sanger models. Known overlap was removed according to the source annotation; independent donor identity was not re-established by genotype matching.

### Shared gene universe and operational dependency classes

Broad portal feature labels and Sanger gene labels were used as the gene-symbol join keys. The allowed universe was the 16,691 GeneSymbol entries in the Broad source-data sheet “Fig5b diff deps volcano,” representing its published non-common-essential comparison universe. Inner joins and complete values across the three aggregate profiles yielded 15,008 genes. Missing gene effects were not imputed. No cross-resource conversion of absolute score scales was fitted.

For each profile, stronger dependency corresponded to a more negative processed effect. Strongest-decile membership was defined as a value at or below that profile’s empirical 0.10 quantile, including ties. Let B3, S3 and B2 denote membership in the Broad 3D, Sanger 3D and Broad 2D strongest deciles. The three clinical comparison classes were B2∩B3∩S3 (UNIVERSAL_2D_BOTH3D), B3∩S3 outside B2 (THREE_D_SPECIFIC_CONSENSUS), and B2 outside both B3 and S3 (TWO_D_ONLY). Remaining patterns were labelled OTHER. These terms refer to aggregate ranks, not cell-by-cell essentiality or a formal differential-dependency test. Previously executed q5 and q20 classifications are descriptive sensitivities.

### Overlap and concordance statistics

Overlap of the two 3D strongest-decile sets was evaluated using a one-sided Fisher exact test over the 15,008-gene universe. The odds ratio compares co-membership with membership in only one or neither set. The original numerical implementation underflowed for the smallest overlap P values; zero-valued output is not interpreted as an exact zero probability. Spearman correlation was used for the primary profile comparison and Pearson correlation as a descriptive companion.

For the difference in Spearman correlations, the existing workflow used 2,000 bootstrap draws of gene rows with replacement, with NumPy random seed 20260919. The three values for a gene remained aligned in each draw. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of finite 3D-minus-2D differences formed the reported interval. This is a conditional gene-resampling interval, not a model-, donor- or pathway-clustered interval. No equivalence margin was specified.

### Historical clinical-precedence annotation and regression

Open Targets release 26.06 provided target symbols, essentiality annotations, the disease hierarchy and clinical-precedence evidence [10]. CRC comprised MONDO_0005575 and its descendants, a set of 88 disease identifiers in this release. Evidence was joined through target identifiers to approved gene symbols, and each gene received the maximum coded stage among its CRC-linked records. Unmatched or unknown stages were coded as zero. The locked ordering was UNKNOWN=0, PRECLINICAL=0.1, IND=0.5, EARLY_PHASE_1=0.75, PHASE_1=1, PHASE_1_2=1.5, PHASE_2=2, PHASE_2_3=2.5, PHASE_3=3, PREAPPROVAL=3.5, and APPROVAL, PHASE_4 or WITHDRAWAL=4. Phase II-or-higher and Phase III-or-higher indicators used cut-offs of 2 and 3. The highest-stage indicator was retained only to disclose approval-endpoint sparsity, not to assert verified current regulatory approval.

Unadjusted class comparisons used two-sided Fisher exact tests. The adjusted q10 comparison included only UNIVERSAL_2D_BOTH3D and TWO_D_ONLY. For gene g, the model was logit{Pr(Yg=1)}=α+βR·Rg+βB·Zg+βE·Eg. Rg was 1 for the all-three-profile class and 0 for TWO_D_ONLY. Zg was the continuous Broad CRC 2D gene effect standardized within the 994-gene comparison set using the population standard deviation (ddof=0); a lower value represented stronger dependency. Eg was the locked Open Targets-derived binary indicator obtained by checking for any true isEssential flag in geneEssentiality records, aggregating by symbol, and coding missing values as false. This operational variable is not a measurement of universal essentiality across the CRC cohorts.

Binomial generalized linear models with a logit link were fitted using statsmodels. Odds ratios were exp(βR), and Wald 95% CIs were exp(βR±1.96·SE). No tractability, development-investment, gene-family or study-intensity covariates were added. No alternative penalized model was substituted for the unstable approval fit. Nominal P values are presented without a new multiplicity correction; the positive secondary endpoint is not treated as confirmatory evidence.

### Endpoint-orientation correction

During internal audit, a formula-based Boolean outcome was found to invert the event orientation in an earlier exploratory model. The locked q10 comparison was therefore refitted with an explicit numeric 0/1 endpoint without changing the comparison set, threshold, disease scope or covariates. The successful correction asserted 645 all-three-profile and 349 TWO_D_ONLY genes, with 45 Phase II-or-higher, 30 Phase III-or-higher and three highest-stage events. The numerical endpoint values were 0 and 1. The sorted gene/class/endpoint payload had SHA-256 6916208707fb7779916359a6ccc5f61d5db306f435787d5328fdd4df77185bdb. This fingerprint covers the specified identifiers and endpoints, not the entire raw-data collection or every covariate. The corrected numeric-endpoint estimates are the only adjusted clinical estimates used for conclusions in this manuscript; earlier files and logs are retained for traceability.

### PDO DSS processing and target mapping

The independent pharmacology input was Data S5.xlsx from Mendeley Data version 3 associated with Kryeziu et al. [9]. The workbook contained 211 rows and 62 columns. The frozen parser excluded metadata columns sample_id, run_id_harmonize, run_id_lib1 and run_id_lib2; it removed the named regimen labels FLIRI, FLOX, FLV, MAPK3X, 5FU–gemcitabine and volasertib–SN38 after alphanumeric normalization, and labels containing an ampersand. Columns sharing a base label after removal of _lib1 or _lib2 were averaged within each PDO row over available numeric values. The resulting non-missing values were summarized as a median DSS for each retained drug label. The parser’s 51-series output is an operational single-agent-labelled set, not a newly curated proof that every label represents a uniquely selective molecule. It was not restricted to the 24-drug harmonized panel, and run identifiers were not included in a batch model.

Names, synonyms and trade names were mapped to Open Targets drug_molecule records using normalized alphanumeric labels. Ambiguous aliases were not accepted as unique matches. A frozen small synonym dictionary was used as documented in the original workflow. Drug_mechanism_of_action records were linked to target symbols, retaining action labels matching inhibitory, antagonistic, blocking, degrading, negative, suppressive or disruptive terms. Duplicate drug–molecule–gene tuples were removed. Targets were then restricted to the common dependency universe. These are database mechanism annotations, not newly measured engagement or biochemical selectivity at the PDO exposure levels.

### Drug-level pharmacologic association

For each of the 26 retained drugs and each dependency resource, effect values were multiplied by −1 so that greater values indicated stronger dependency. The strongest-target summary was the maximum magnitude among that drug’s retained mapped targets; the median-target summary was their median. A drug’s maximizing target could differ between resources. Spearman correlations related these summaries to median PDO DSS across drugs. The comparison of Broad 3D and 2D used the paired difference of correlations, not a comparison of their separate significance labels.

The existing workflow used 10,000 bootstrap resamples of drug rows with replacement, preserving each drug’s DSS and resource summaries together. The NumPy generator was initialized with seed 20260919, and 95% intervals were percentile intervals of finite differences. Leave-one-drug-out ranges were computed for sensitivity. The same procedures were already applied to the 21-drug subset with at most three targets retained in the common universe; that count does not denote a complete off-target inventory. Drug resampling did not cluster by patient or shared mechanism. Preliminary target-level analyses involved seven single-target genes and 72 genes with inclusive inhibitory annotations; their full existing results are designated exploratory in the supplement.

## Figure legends

## Figure 1. Resource selection and distinct validation layers

The study compares aggregate dependency profiles from 18 Broad CRC 3D screens, 67 lineage-matched Broad adherent 2D screens and 81 Sanger CRC models after exclusion of annotated Broad overlap. The Broad published Figure 5 comparison universe contained 16,691 gene symbols; complete values shared by all three profiles defined the 15,008-gene analysis universe. Historical clinical-precedence analyses used Open Targets 26.06 and 88 CRC disease identifiers. Independent pharmacology originated from a 211-PDO-row, 62-column DSS workbook; the preferred analysis comprised 26 drugs. Statistical units are genes for cross-profile and clinical comparisons and drugs for pharmacologic association. Cohort sizes denote source screens or models, not patient counts. This descriptive flow diagram contains no hypothesis test or CI and does not imply paired identical models or patient-level inference.

## Figure 2. Strongest-decile overlap and its 2D component

Each 3D strongest-decile list contained 1,501 genes from the 15,008-gene shared universe. The 763-gene intersection comprised 645 genes also in the Broad 2D strongest decile and 118 outside it; 349 additional genes were in Broad 2D but outside both 3D strongest-decile lists. The all-three-profile fraction of the 3D intersection was 84.53%. The two-3D co-membership OR was 17.8883 from a one-sided Fisher exact test. The stored overlap P value underflowed numerically; an exact P value is therefore not reported. No OR CI was estimated in the locked overlap analysis. Unit: gene. Categories describe aggregate rank membership, not universal essentiality, culture-format interactions or therapeutic target specificity. The displayed categories are limited to the membership counts in the locked output.

## Figure 3. Cross-resource concordance against a lineage-matched 2D comparator

Spearman correlations across 15,008 genes were 0.3299897370 for Broad CRC 3D versus Sanger CRC 3D and 0.3531986912 for Broad CRC 2D versus Sanger CRC 3D. The directly evaluated difference was −0.0232089542; the conditional gene-bootstrap 95% interval was −0.0330344 to −0.0131187, using 2,000 aligned gene resamples. Pearson companion correlations were 0.5109250 and 0.5181936. Bootstrap intervals were estimated for the correlation difference, not for individual correlations. Cohort sizes were 18 and 67 Broad screens and 81 Sanger models. Unit: gene conditional on fixed cohort-mean profiles. The figure does not estimate a randomized or within-model culture-format effect or establish biological superiority of 2D.

## Figure 4. Historical clinical precedence with corrected event orientation

Unadjusted Phase II-or-higher frequencies were 36/645 for the all-three-profile class and 9/349 for TWO_D_ONLY; Phase III-or-higher frequencies were 27/645 and 3/349. Their two-sided Fisher ORs were 2.2332 and 5.0388, with P=0.03661 and P=0.002901. In the separate exclusive-3D comparison, frequencies were 4/118 versus 9/349 for Phase II-or-higher (OR, 1.3255; P=0.7461), and 0/118 versus 3/349 for Phase III-or-higher (P=0.5751). The adjusted model used 994 gene rows, explicit numeric outcomes, all-three-profile membership, standardized Broad 2D effect and the Open Targets-derived essentiality indicator. Corrected adjusted ORs were 1.3487761406 (Wald 95% CI, 0.5853139188–3.1080707618; P=0.4823844923) and 4.2187090214 (1.1886118578–14.9733537404; nominal P=0.0259240472), with 45 and 30 events. The original highest-stage/approval-coded endpoint had three events and an unstable separated fit; it is described by event counts rather than an interpretable OR. Unit: gene. Secondary retrospective endpoints; no multiplicity-adjusted or causal interpretation. The adjusted contrast is not exclusive-3D versus 2D-only. Correction trace: run 35414237376.

## Figure 5. Exploratory independent PDO pharmacologic association

For 26 mapped inhibitory drugs, strongest-target dependency correlations with median PDO DSS were 0.4200791 (P=0.03263), 0.2947730 (P=0.14378) and 0.3176291 (P=0.11383) for Broad CRC 3D, Broad CRC 2D and Sanger CRC 3D, respectively. The Broad 3D-minus-2D difference was +0.1253061 (10,000-draw paired drug-bootstrap 95% interval, −0.08612 to +0.36223). Median-target correlations were 0.2428916, 0.1959577 and −0.0236423, respectively, with a Broad difference of +0.0469339 (−0.17130 to +0.26653). Existing at-most-three-retained-target sensitivities used 21 drugs and are detailed in Supplementary Table S6. Unit: drug; DSS is a cohort median over available PDO rows. The starting workbook had 211 PDO rows, not 211 independent patients. Intervals resample drugs, not patients or target families. The selected strongest target can differ by resource. Neither separate correlation P values nor positive leave-one-drug-out point estimates establish a between-resource advantage. All pharmacologic comparisons are exploratory.
